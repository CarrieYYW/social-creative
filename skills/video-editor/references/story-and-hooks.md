# Story and hooks

Use before choosing a template, treatment or edit for any new video. Settle what the viewer should want to know and how the video answers it. Evidence, limits and rejected claims are in [story research](story-research-2026-11-05.md).

Nobody can engineer a viral video. Luck and distribution play a large part. A strong video raises the odds and earns its result; repeat attempts do the rest. Never promise virality or predict a score.

## Write the story first

Fill in the project's `story` block before polishing. If a field stays blank, the video has a story problem that better editing will not fix.

| Field | Answer in one line |
|---|---|
| `audience` | Who exactly is this for? |
| `takeaway` | What should they remember tomorrow? |
| `hookType` | Which opening below? |
| `promise` | What does the opening make them want to know? |
| `payoff` | Where and how is that answered? |
| `shareReason` | What is someone saying when they send this? |

## The hook that works for every video

No single line works everywhere. The structure does. Every strong opening does three jobs at once, within about the first second or two:

1. **Make the subject clear.** The viewer knows what this is and that it is for them. Unclear openings were the one hook feature tied to fewer likes in the only large study of openings found.
2. **Open a real question.** Show a gap the viewer wants closed: a contradiction, a result without its cause, a feeling they recognise, something unfinished. Curiosity needs a gap that is neither obvious nor impossible to guess.
3. **Say it through every channel.** First frame, on-screen words and first spoken line carry the same idea. Do not make them compete.

As a sentence: **show [who or what this is for] in [a state of tension] so the viewer needs [the payoff].** Then pay it off honestly.

Start with the subject or situation. Skip empty channel introductions, greetings, logos or fades that delay it. An in-scene greeting can stay when the first frame already establishes the situation; judge it in the complete opening. A template's first frame must already show the hook, which is why Studio warns on `empty-open` and `hook-late`.

## Choose the hook type

These are starting points, not fill-in-the-blank lines. Use the brand's own voice.

| Type | Mechanism | Loud version | Quiet version |
|---|---|---|---|
| `result-first` | Show the end, withhold how | "We sold out in nine minutes." | The finished room, then how it began |
| `contradiction` | Break a belief they hold | "You don't need more discipline." | "Rest isn't the opposite of work." |
| `recognition` | Name something they feel but have not said | "If you reread texts before sending them…" | One exact, private-feeling line |
| `question` | Ask what they already wonder | "Why do Sundays feel like this?" | A question left open on screen |
| `stakes` | Show what is at risk or running out | "Forty tickets left." | "This is the last summer in this building." |
| `mid-action` | Drop in as something happens | Mid-sentence, mid-movement | A hand already reaching for the door |
| `image` | One picture that needs explaining | An arresting or odd frame | One still, strange, beautiful image |

A quiet brand can hook hard. A hook needs tension, not volume. Silence, stillness and one exact sentence can open a gap as well as a shout.

## Check the hook

- With sound off, is the subject clear from the first frame and words?
- With eyes closed, does the first spoken line still work?
- Does the viewer know who this is for?
- Is there a question they want answered?
- Does the video actually answer it? A fake gap costs trust and later attention.
- Could a competitor post this unchanged? If so, make it more specific.

## Hold attention after the hook

- **Link beats with "but" or "therefore", never "and then".** Read the outline aloud. If "and then" fits between beats, the beats do not depend on each other.
- **Give every beat one job.** Roles: hook, setup, turn, explain, example, payoff, close. Cut any beat that does not change what the viewer knows or feels.
- **Vary the emotion.** Open with surprise, then use short returns of warmth or delight rather than one sustained note. Viewers adapt to anything constant.
- **Keep pace moderate.** More cuts and more intensity is not automatically better. Pace is a means of reading, not a goal.
- **Hold a complete story.** Setup, rising tension, turn, resolution. Even 15 seconds can have all four.
- **End when the payoff lands.** Do not add a recap after it. A clear next action is fine; a long sign-off is not.
- **Place the brand by goal.** Sharing tends to suffer when a brand arrives early and interrupts the story. Recall and sales guidance says the opposite. Decide which goal this video serves.

## Design for the send

Sharing says something about the sharer. Name which of these the video gives them.

| When someone sends it, they mean | Built from |
|---|---|
| "This is so you" / "This is us" | Recognition and specificity |
| "You need to see this" | Awe, surprise, delight |
| "This will help you" | Practical value that works without the rest of the feed |
| "This is what I believe" | A clear position, stated well |
| "We should go" | An invitation with the details included |

Use awe, amusement, warmth and inspiration. Anger and anxiety also spread, but manufactured outrage or fear is not an accepted method here.

## Structure by video type

Pick the structure from the type. Each maps to a Studio template.

| Type | Story engine | Beat order | Template |
|---|---|---|---|
| Talking head | One argument from one person | Claim that surprises → why it matters → proof or example → the shift → what to do | `talking-head` |
| Testimonial | Before and after in one voice | Moment it changed → before → what happened → now | `talking-head` |
| Motion explainer | Make an invisible idea visible | Hook → setup → turn → mechanism → example → payoff | `explainer` |
| Quote | One thought, given room | Setup phrase → turn phrase → hold. Reveal at reading pace | `quote-editorial`, `quote-poster` |
| Event promo | The feeling of being there | Cold open on the best moment → arrival → people → details speed up → peak → one breath → title → date and place | `event-promo` |
| Event recap | Proof it happened | Same acts, ending on "next time" instead of date | `event-promo` |
| Narrated clips | A voice carries, footage shows | Line → image that adds to it, never repeats it | `talking-head` with B-roll over the whole voice, until a dedicated template exists |
| Demo or how-to | Watch it work | Finished result first → steps → result again | Not yet a template |

For footage-led work, story lives in clip order. Assign each clip a job before cutting: establish context, show action or detail, show a reaction or discovery, pay it off. Shot length follows the job: wide openers hold, details are short, and a slower shot after the peak gives the viewer room to feel it.

## Test openings properly

Keep the body fixed and change only the opening. In Studio, add entries to `variants` and render each with `--variant`. Record the hypothesis and result using the project's creative-testing route. An opening test changes first frame, text and spoken line together, so it tests the package rather than one element.

A retention drop shows where to look, not why people left.
