# Project Visual and Sound Treatment

Use for repeatable visual/editorial direction across a series or when references need to become production decisions. Keep it with the active project's existing reference library or brand brief. A one-off edit can use a concise treatment in the working brief; it does not need a new folder system.

## Keep a compact, contextual guide

Choose a few representative examples and explain what each demonstrates. A large unannotated inspiration dump is less useful than examples tied to decisions. Record audience/message fit, sources, inspection coverage, explicit preferences, and rejected alternatives. Distinguish a campaign treatment from a brand-wide rule.

Capture only what changes the result:

- **Typography and captions:** roles for titles, captions, labels, emphasis, and attribution; fonts, line breaks, scale relationships, contrast, holds, and placement against actual footage and destination overlays.
- **Scene vocabulary:** when to use the speaker, supporting graphics, a split composition, full-frame explanation, demonstration, or a deliberate pause. Choose from meaning rather than requiring every layout in every edit.
- **Motion and imagery:** what changes, what that change explains, pacing range, continuity, and appropriate image treatment. Quiet motion and still holds are valid choices.
- **Longer-form structure:** chapter transitions, recurring graphic language, local versus overall narrative rhythm, and how the ending resolves the larger piece. Short-form treatments can emphasize standalone context and a compact arc instead.
- **Sound:** the role of voice, music, ambience, effects, and silence; useful examples and when they do or do not fit.

Use actual source copy and a representative sequence to test the treatment. Static quote/carousel work can share type, palette, imagery, and visual motifs, but it needs its own reading arc; a slide sequence is not automatically an effective video. Review UX/communication and UI/craft separately before extending the system.

## Index the assets and sound palette

Reuse supplied or appropriately licensed assets. Keep a small manifest when it helps production:

| Asset / path | Role and suitable context | Source / rights / destination limits | Timing or treatment notes |
|---|---|---|---|

An effect's label is not enough: audition the actual file. An indexed sound palette may group transitions, interface cues, textures, accents, or anticipation/resolution sounds when those fit the project; there is no required count or category. Store alternatives and reasons to avoid a sound where useful. Do not treat collected sounds from someone else's edits as cleared for reuse.

For a substantial sequence, add sound cues to the existing beat plan: beat/time, event or purpose, asset, intended onset/duration, and its relationship to voice/music. Match audible emphasis to meaning or visible action and retain intentional silence. Do not add a sound for every moving object or copy a universal gain value.

## Keep the guide useful

Retain the representative artifact, fixed constraints, feedback, actual outcomes when available, and why a rule applies. A preference or attractive reference is not evidence of retention. Revise the guide from explicit feedback and actual use; avoid carrying another project's colors, voice, music, or tempo into a new brand silently. See [Sound for Video](audio-design.md) for listening, mixing, measurement, and export checks.
